# Physics evidence and calibration notes

This document records the scope of measurements used to guide the prototype. Matching a few reference values is not validation of a complete cricket simulator. Values are in metres and seconds unless stated otherwise; the trajectory sampler is deterministic for a fixed delivery. Other game systems still use randomness.

## Incoming pace delivery at the pitch

[James and colleagues, *The playing performance of county cricket pitches* (2004)](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=dfa2679a9454ecf27a4fb17157412087db5e5b71&repid=rep1&type=pdf) analysed roughly 3,000 impacts by 82 professional players. For two example pitches, the paper's fitted rebound speeds at a 36 m/s incoming speed correspond to about 87.6% and 90.0% retained **total** speed. These examples describe particular pitches and conditions; they are not a universal bound, and the paper notes that spun-ball rebounds are more variable and difficult to assess in two dimensions.

The current game uses **0.9 retained longitudinal speed** at impact for pace deliveries as an initial approximation. Its existing vertical rebound factor remains 0.55, and lateral movement is still authored. With a centred fast delivery, middle pace, over the wicket and bounce targets at z = −7.5, −3.5 and −1.5 m, the instantaneous total-speed ratios in the regression fixture are approximately 0.890, 0.889 and 0.887 respectively. The fixture allows 0.86–0.92 to account for finite sampling and geometry. This compares different impact speeds and a simplified model to the study examples; it is a plausibility check, not a fit or a claim of measured accuracy.

The selected duration still represents the whole release-to-crease flight. The model advances faster before the bounce and slower after it so that the selected bounce point and total duration remain unchanged. Swing, spin, the pitch sound, the contact point and the projected wicket all use the same delivery-time mapping. Spin currently retains all longitudinal speed as an explicit placeholder: the seam-bowling data above should not be applied to spin without suitable evidence. Air drag, seam orientation, changing pitch conditions and secondary bounces are not yet calibrated.

## Bat impact and outgoing motion — evidence and implementation gap

[Peploe et al. (2018), *The relationships between impact location and post-impact ball speed, bat torsion, and ball direction in cricket batting*](https://www.stuartmcnaylor.com/publication/CB2IL/Peploe_et_al_2017.pdf) measured 239 dynamic shots from 20 male batters, club through international standard. Instantaneous post-impact ball speed averaged 28.1 ± 4.2 m/s (range 17.8–39.5 m/s). Absolute impact offsets from the bat-face midline and sweetspot were negatively associated with speed; a combined location regression had adjusted R² = 0.48 and standard error 2.99 m/s. Impacts within 2 cm of the midline and 4.5 cm longitudinally from the sweetspot reduced speed by less than 6%; direction deviations within 2 cm of the midline were under 10° from the intended target. These are population/task findings, not universal coefficients or guaranteed outcomes.

[Peploe et al. (2018), range-hitting study](https://oars.uos.ac.uk/786/) assessed 20 batters and reports that bat speed at impact plus medio-lateral and longitudinal impact location explained 68% of observed instantaneous post-impact ball-speed variation. The authors' [accepted manuscript](https://www.stuartmcnaylor.com/publication/CB3tech/Peploe_et_al_2019.pdf) reports 239 forward-directed trials; its 3.5–101.5 m carry span is modeled carry, not directly measured landing distance. A [2022 biomechanics synthesis](https://www.stuartmcnaylor.com/publication/cricket_BASES/McErlain-Naylor_et_al_2022.pdf) explicitly notes that bat angle and impact-location factors still need further investigation in its power-hitting model.

These sources support which variables matter; they do not define a universal response for HardHit. `BatBallContactEvent` records estimated along/across face offsets in metres, blade-point velocity at the collision location, and distal-tip velocity immediately before contact. Across is world-X relative to the pivot, clamped to the blade width; face roll is not modeled. Collision and Babylon animation share keyframes, and collision derives the handed pivot from the rendered local anchor and stance yaw. The v2 swing curve was adjusted so the modeled distal speed at a well-timed central contact is 26.8 m/s, matching the 2019 study's mean bat speed at impact (20 male batters; 239 forward-directed shots). This is a calibration anchor, not independent validation. Deterministic geometry/velocity fixtures and browser match/replay cover the implementation; two static v3 impact poses have since been visually reviewed as described below.

The 2019 range-hitting paper's third in-sample stepwise model predicts launch speed as `12.587 + 0.772 × batSpeed − 109.693 × |X| − 35.584 × |Z|` in SI units. `X` is medio-lateral distance from the sweetspot and `Z` is longitudinal distance from it; the sweetspot is reported 17.5 cm from the toe. The fitted model explains 67.6% of training variation (SEE 2.35 m/s) across 239 forward-directed trials from 20 male batters. `src/bat-impact-response.ts` implements this equation as an isolated diagnostic candidate: its along-blade coordinate is measured from the shoulder, its across coordinate is signed about the midline, and the input bat speed is estimated distal-tip speed. At the study mean of 26.8 m/s, a modelled sweetspot impact predicts 33.28 m/s; at the same speed with offsets X=2 cm and Z=5 cm it predicts 29.31 m/s. These are equation evaluations, not a fit to the unavailable per-trial dataset.

A separate 2021 study of 15 male and 15 female batters is an external group-level plausibility check: maximum bat speed averaged 28.4 ± 2.5 and 22.6 ± 2.3 m/s, while launch speed averaged 33.5 ± 2.6 and 27.3 ± 2.8 m/s, respectively. The candidate predicts 34.51 and 30.03 m/s for centered impacts at those respective bat speeds, within one reported launch-speed standard deviation of each group mean. The study summaries do not pair those variables at the shot level, so this is not individual or holdout validation. Keep the authored gameplay launch response unchanged. Direction, elevation, spin, face roll, broader visual impact-pose review, independent per-shot holdout data, and full flight/carry calibration remain separate work; do not tune the strict-six fixture to one outcome.

### V3 measurement mapping and study scope — November 15, 2026

The [original range-hitting manuscript](https://www.stuartmcnaylor.com/publication/CB3tech/Peploe_et_al_2019.pdf) defines bat speed as the resultant speed of the midpoint of two distal blade markers at impact. The game's `batTipVelocity` is a finite-difference velocity of the modeled toe, including v3 lateral translation, so it approximates the same kind of distal point; it is not a measured marker trajectory. The study derived impact coordinates and post-impact velocity from ball motion and a three-dimensional bat frame. The game's coordinates instead use a closest point on a rotating blade segment and a world-X offset clamped to the modeled width; face roll and bat thickness are absent. A sphere/segment overlap at an edge can therefore yield a clamped coordinate without establishing where the physical ball would strike the face. Treat the candidate's output as diagnostic only.

The 239 analyzed trials came from forward-projected range shots by 20 male batters facing a full-length bowling-machine delivery, with inbound speed at impact 25.0 ± 1.3 m/s. The paper's carry values came from a flight model rather than measured landing locations. These conditions do not establish prediction accuracy for arbitrary game delivery speeds, lengths, directions or batter populations. The vertical launch-angle model was fitted to a subset of 99 impacts within 2 cm of the midline; the all-trial angle regression did not satisfy the paper's residual-normality condition. Neither equation provides an independent holdout for the other. The accessible manuscript and repository record describe aggregate results; this review did not find a usable per-shot dataset. Do not route either regression into gameplay without independent shot-level validation and a defensible mapping of the missing face orientation.

Static rendered contact poses for both hands were inspected for one off-line fast delivery from a custom oblique camera after the v3 reach fix. The enlarged visual ball was centered on the modeled ball for that review. This checks visible placement for those two poses, not motion accuracy, marker measurement agreement, face roll or human playability.

## Automated running between wickets

The seeded bot simulation and live scene advance fielders toward each successive ball sample using the same reaction threshold, movement speed and catch/ground-stop checks. Earlier bot distributions used a straight-line reach envelope from each fielder's starting position, so they are not comparable to the corrected chase. A later correction allows reachable airborne catches below 1.2 m; the old height cutoff incorrectly excluded them. For seed `0x51a7` and 80 overs per tier, the default-layout simulation produces 163/154/116 ground stops, including 158/149/103 recoveries after rest, and 2/4/1 catches for Rookie/Medium/Hard; each tier has 480 legal balls. Earlier distributions excluded balls retrieved after rest and are not directly comparable. These synthetic results expose current behavior, not calibrated fielding or a target distribution; all reachable catches are currently successful and diving, fielding control and catch difficulty are not modeled.

[Lockie and colleagues, *Analysis of Specific Speed Testing for Cricketers* (2013)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23439342/) report a mean 2.83 ± 0.15 s for a 17.68 m sprint with a bat (n=17 experienced male cricketers in the reported table). Dividing distance by mean time gives about 6.25 m/s as a mean-equivalent speed; this is not a measured constant-speed profile and does not describe all ages, genders, skill levels or match decisions. The test starts with the bat at a crease and slides it through the far crease.

MCC Law 30.1 defines a batter as out of their ground unless part of their person or bat is grounded behind the popping crease; the wicket itself is on the bowling crease. HardHit's modeled popping creases are at z=±8.8 m and stumps at z=±10 m. The runner model now travels 17.6 m between those modeled safe lines and uses the evidence-derived mean-equivalent 6.25 m/s, while a return throw still targets the stumps. That predicts about 2.82 s for one straight crease-to-crease run, close to the cited single-sprint mean. This uses the scene's rounded coordinates rather than the study's 17.68 m and remains a first-order model.

For repeated runs, [Lockie, Jeffries and Callaghan's run-a-three study (2012)](https://sjss.sportsacademy.edu.rs/archive/getarchive/390) reports mean total times of 9.74 s and 10.38 s for equally sized faster and slower groups (16 experienced male cricketers in total). Their combined group mean is 10.06 s. Using the game's 17.6 m and 6.25 m/s gives 8.448 s for three uninterrupted crossings; the remaining 1.612 s divided across two turns suggests about 0.8 s per turn. The model now holds both runners at the crease for that provisional duration between crossings. This estimate combines a single-run speed from one small study with a total from another; it is a simplified timing fit, not a measured stationary turn or independent validation. The study's reported turn interval includes running five metres on each side of the crease and must not be added as an extra stationary delay.

The batter models now move along the same numerical crossing timeline; on a fielded return they continue to complete the adjudicated runs or move toward the nearest safe creases. Strides are schematic, and motion on the uncommon fallback shots is illustrative because those runs remain distance estimates. The model otherwise assumes constant speed and does not animate grounded bat/body contact, deceleration, acceleration or individual runner differences. In a return-throw race it assumes the nearer runner can turn back to safety at the same speed; the throw and gather are also provisional. Its 10.05 s three-run time is a model-derived comparison to the sample mean, not proof of realistic match decisions. Source law: [MCC Laws, 2017 Code, 4th Edition (2026), Law 30](https://www.lords.org/getmedia/a4ef9f77-2a25-4f5b-a286-4601f08e6334/Laws-of-Cricket-2017-Code-4th-Edition-%282026%29_5.pdf).

## Aerodynamics and remaining evidence

Pad interception and missed-delivery wicket adjudication now use one sampled position from the authored delivery path at the stance-adjusted batter plane. This prevents those two checks from drifting due to separate path calculations, but it does not calibrate the path, pad geometry, first-contact order or LBW projection; pad deflection and measured delivery/body data remain open.

[Stretch et al., “The force absorption and rebound characteristics of cricket batting pads at four impact velocities” (1998)](https://www.distantreader.org/stacks/journals/sajsm/sajsm-758.pdf) tested four pad types at 15.34, 22.97, 30.68 and 34.45 m/s. Reported mean rebound distances range from 7.16–7.84 m at the slowest speed, 8.01–9.27 m at the next, 10.07–11.06 m at the third, and 10.89–12.52 m at the highest, depending on pad type. The study's rebound test projected a 156 g ball at an approximately 45° approach onto a pad fixed vertically above astroturf and measured distance along the pitch. It does not provide a generic 3D post-impact velocity vector or coefficient of restitution. Therefore these data establish pad- and speed-dependent rebound behavior, but cannot alone define a transferable in-game response law; scene geometry and outgoing angle still need an explicit, testable mapping or new measurements.

The current provisional visual chooses the P2 series (7.44, 8.11, 10.07, 10.89 m). For the study's 45° incidence and a same-height ballistic return, it infers the normal restitution as `e = reboundDistance × g / impactSpeed²`, since the normal and vertical velocity components are equal in that idealized setup. It linearly interpolates `e` by total impact speed, reflects the normal component at the in-game vertical pad, preserves tangential components (frictionless), and then advances the ball with gravity and the existing air/ground model. This reconstructs the four source ranges by construction; it does not validate rebound direction, friction, arbitrary incidence, other pad designs, or a real batter's moving pad.

The [Cambridge review of cricket-ball swing](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/flow/article/review-and-reassessment-of-the-aerodynamics-of-cricket-ball-swing/389B2E66A420FF51FAB3EC2186AFE0AE) describes sensitivity to ball condition, seam, flight and air conditions. Its example of a 4 m/s crosswind changing lateral displacement by about 0.18 m is a model result for a particular delivery, not a universal swing limit. The game's swing slider is an authored approximation; no wind, seam state or spin-dependent aerodynamics have been validated.

Before claiming cricket-grade fidelity, gather appropriately licensed, independent delivery trajectories and bat/ball contact data; specify source, uncertainty and tolerance for each fixture; verify both hands and bowling arms; and compare prediction to measurements not used for tuning. Record limitations when a measurement or physical device is unavailable.
